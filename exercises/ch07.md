**Built-in notebook close (`ch07-build-it-break-it.ipynb`):** this notebook ends not with a scaffold but with a question: after seeing LIME- and SHAP-guided attacks beat random editing, "when two explanations disagree, how would you have known which to trust?" (Chapter 8's job).

On this clean corpus LIME and SHAP tie (the notebook says so); in the real study the notebook cites, LIME-guided attacks cut detector recall from 0.81 to 0.24 while SHAP-guided only reached 0.65. ## Exercise 7.1: Flip rate vs edit budget *(warm-up)*

**Goal.** The attacker's budget is hard-coded at `MAX_EDITS=2` ("stealthy"). Measure how each ranker's flip rate grows with budget, and where random editing catches up.

**Steps.**
1. In the attack-loop cell, wrap the `summary` computation in a loop over `MAX_EDITS in [1, 2, 4, 8]`.
2. Plot flip rate vs budget, one line per ranker (random / LIME / SHAP), plus mean edit rate.
3. Note the budget at which random editing's flip rate becomes non-trivial.

**Hint.** `MAX_EDITS` is a module-level constant read inside `attack()`; either make it a parameter or reassign it before each sweep iteration.

**Solution sketch.** ~6 lines around existing code. At budget 1, only rankers that put the strong tell first can flip anything; random needs enough edits to hit the tell by luck (each machine text hides 1 strong tell in ~17 words, so expect random to stay weak until the budget approaches a good fraction of the document). Record your curves and compare against the notebook's budget-2 bar chart. The guided-vs-random *gap as a function of budget* is the cleanest statement of "the explanation is the attack surface."

## Exercise 7.2: Adversarial retraining: does the defense hold? *(core)*

**Goal.** The obvious defense: retrain the detector on attacked texts. Test whether it works, and what it costs on clean accuracy.

**Steps.**
1. Run the LIME-guided attack over the *training* machine texts (not just `caught` test docs) and collect the edited versions, still labeled machine.
2. Retrain the same pipeline on original training data + attacked copies.
3. Re-run the full attack evaluation (random / LIME / SHAP) against the hardened detector, and re-measure clean held-out accuracy.

**Hint.** The attack deleted the tells, so the hardened detector must find *other* signal. But the corpus was built so the neutral bed carries none. Think about what the detector can possibly learn from a tell-less machine text before you run it; the run then confirms or refutes you.

**Solution sketch.** ~15 lines: loop `attack`-style editing over training docs, extend the lists, refit `detector`, rerun the evaluation cells. No committed numbers exist; record what you find. Plausible outcomes worth distinguishing: (a) clean accuracy collapses toward chance because tell-less machine text is indistinguishable by construction, or (b) the detector latches onto the weak tells or the substituted `"the"` frequency itself (a new shortcut, which a fresh LIME pass will expose). Either result deepens the chapter's point: transparency governs the *arms race*, not just the first attack.

## Exercise 7.3: Make SHAP lose (or win) on purpose *(stretch)*

**Goal.** The notebook's honest note: LIME and SHAP tie here because there is exactly one tell, but on real nonlinear detectors SHAP spread credit across correlated tokens and guided the weaker attack. Recreate that mechanism synthetically: make the tells *correlated* and see the explainers come apart.

**Steps.**
1. In the corpus cell, change `machine_text()` so the strong tell is a correlated *pair*: e.g., always insert two of `["delve", "moreover", "comprehensive"]` together (pick the pair per document), instead of one strong + independent weak tells.
2. Rebuild corpus, retrain the detector, confirm held-out accuracy is still high.
3. Re-run the three-ranker attack at `MAX_EDITS=2` and compare LIME vs SHAP flip rates; inspect a few rankings to see whether SHAP splits credit across the pair while LIME concentrates on one member.

**Hint.** The attack budget matters: with credit split over 2 co-occurring tells, a ranker that puts *both* in its top-2 wins; a ranker that interleaves decoys loses. Also check `rank_shap`'s positive-value filter: split credit can push individual values near zero.

**Solution sketch.** ~10 changed lines in the generator, then rerun. Unledgered; record what you find and compare against the study numbers quoted in the notebook (LIME 0.24 vs SHAP 0.65 post-attack recall on the real detector). Whichever explainer wins on your variant, you will have shown the notebook's closing lesson mechanically: explainers are not interchangeable, and which one is "better" depends on how the model distributes its evidence.
