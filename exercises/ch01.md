## Exercise 1.1: Does the opaque model confess too? *(warm-up)*

**Goal.** The chapter's story is "the transparent LR warned us, the GBT didn't." But the GBT is only silent because nobody asked it. Test whether permutation importance makes the GBT confess the same shortcut.

**Steps.**
1. Find the cell that trains `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` on the dense TF-IDF features.
2. After it, add `from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance` and run it on the *iid test* set (`n_repeats=10, random_state=SEED`).
3. Print the top-5 features by mean importance, next to the LR's top-5 coefficients.

**Hint.** Permutation importance needs the dense matrix and the fitted model; reuse the same vectorizer output the GBT was trained on. Compare feature *rankings*, not magnitudes; importances and coefficients are on different scales.

**Solution sketch.**
```python
r = permutation_importance(gbt, X_iid_dense, y_iid, n_repeats=10, random_state=SEED)
top = np.argsort(r.importances_mean)[::-1][:5]
print([(feature_names[j], round(r.importances_mean[j], 3)) for j in top])
```
The LR run put ±10.6 on *romance*/*horror* (4x the best sentiment word, 37.9% of total |coef| mass on 2 of 64 features). Record whether the genre tokens also top the GBT's permutation ranking, and what that does to the chapter's "only one model told us" framing. The honest refinement is that transparency is about *asking*, not just architecture.

## Exercise 1.2: Reproduce the oracle, then move the ceiling *(core)*

**Goal.** The ledger's oracle (LR retrained on decorrelated data) scores 0.827 on the shifted set, close to the analytic ceiling for 3 adjective slots at `ALIGN=0.75` (P(at least 2 slots agree) = 0.844). Test that the ceiling moves the way the analysis predicts when you change `ALIGN`.

**Steps.**
1. Locate the corpus-generation cell that defines `ALIGN = 0.75` and the oracle cell that retrains on cue-decorrelated data.
2. Wrap corpus generation + oracle training in a loop over `ALIGN in [0.6, 0.75, 0.9]` (keep the RNG seeded per run).
3. For each setting, compute the analytic ceiling `P(>=2 of 3 slots agree) = a**3 + 3*a**2*(1-a)` and print it next to the oracle's shifted accuracy.

**Hint.** Only the decorrelated (corr=0.50) corpora matter here: you are measuring the genuine signal, so keep the cue neutralized in both train and test.

**Solution sketch.** Three lines per setting: regenerate corpus at the new `ALIGN`, refit `LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)`, score on the shifted test set. At `ALIGN=0.75` you should land near the ledger's 0.827 vs the 0.844 ceiling; at 0.6 and 0.9 record what you find and compare the gap to the ceiling. The takeaway is that "the task was learnable" is a quantitative claim you can verify, not a hand-wave.

## Exercise 1.3: How cheap does a shortcut have to be? *(stretch)*

**Goal.** The chapter plants the cue at 0.95 correlation and the genuine signal at 0.75 alignment. Find the crossover: at what cue correlation does the model stop preferring the shortcut over the real signal?

**Steps.**

3. Plot both against correlation on a shared x-axis.

**Hint.** The interesting comparison point is `MY_CUE_CORR == ALIGN == 0.75`: the cue is now exactly as reliable as each genuine slot, but it is *one token* instead of a noisy 3-slot vote. Does the model still overweight it?

**Solution sketch.** Loop the existing exercise-cell code, collect `(corr, shift_acc, cue_rank)` tuples, plot. The ledger anchors two points: at 0.95 the drop is from 0.950 to 0.515, and at 0.50 there is no shortcut by construction. Where the curve bends in between is not in the ledger. Record what you find and compare against the chapter's two endpoints. Expect the cue to stay in the top-5 coefficients well below 0.95: a single deterministic-ish token is cheaper for the optimizer than aggregating three noisy ones.
