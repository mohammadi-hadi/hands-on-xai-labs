## Exercise 3.1: Neighbor lists need a floor *(warm-up)*

**Goal.** The ledger measured anisotropy 0.269: two *random* GPT-2 token embeddings already have cosine ≈ 0.27. Re-score the chapter's neighbor tables against that floor.

**Steps.**
1. Rerun the embedding-neighbors cell for ` king` and ` Amsterdam` (ledger: ` king` gives ` queen` .657; ` Amsterdam` gives ` Netherlands` .657, but also ` Oslo` .568 and ` Prague` .567).
2. Add a column: cosine minus the 0.269 baseline (recompute the baseline yourself from 1,000 random token pairs to confirm; smoke mode's N=200 gave 0.268, effectively identical).
3. Decide for each neighbor whether it clears, say, 2x the baseline margin of the top neighbor.

**Hint.** The baseline computation is already in the notebook (the anisotropy cell); you only need to subtract and re-read the tables.

**Solution sketch.** One subtraction column. You should see roughly that ` Oslo` at .568 keeps only ~0.30 above floor while ` Netherlands` keeps ~0.39. The "semantic" gap between a right and a wrong capital is much thinner than the raw cosines suggest. The habit to take away: never quote a similarity without its random baseline.

## Exercise 3.2: Map the attention sink across all 12 layers *(core)*

**Goal.** The ledger measured that layer 5 parks 0.75 of its attention (non-first queries) on the first token. Is that layer 5 being lazy, or a model-wide resting position? Extend the measurement to every layer.

**Steps.**
1. In the attention cell (coreference sentence, `output_attentions=True`), you already have all 12 layers of weights.
2. For each layer, compute the mean attention that non-first-position queries assign to position 0, averaged over heads.
3. Plot the 12 values as a bar chart; annotate layer 5 with the ledger's 0.75.
4. Repeat for the logit-lens prompt ("The capital of the Netherlands is") to check prompt-independence.

**Hint.** `outputs.attentions` is a tuple of `(batch, heads, query, key)` tensors; you want `att[l][0, :, 1:, 0].mean()`.

**Solution sketch.** Twelve numbers, one loop. Layer 5 should reproduce 0.75 exactly (deterministic CPU forward pass). The full 12-layer profile is not in the ledger; record what you find and compare against the chapter's single-layer number; the teaching point is that "the model attends to X" claims must be read against a large default-to-first-token bias (Chapter 5 turns this into a formal discount, and the ledger's cross-reference is the attention-sink literature).

## Exercise 3.3: Who is inflating the residual stream? *(stretch)*

**Goal.** The ledger records a 119x norm swell that is front-loaded: one 3.1x jump at block 3, then blocks 4–11 add only ~1.5x, and honestly notes "plausibly a few outlier dimensions; Ch. 10 can dig." Dig now: test the outlier-dimension hypothesis the chapter raises but does not test.

**Steps.**
1. In the residual-norms cell, instead of taking the L2 norm per layer, keep the per-dimension mean absolute value across prompt tokens: a `(13, 768)` matrix.
2. For each layer, compute what fraction of the total squared norm the top-5 dimensions carry.
3. Plot that fraction by layer, and print the indices of the top dimensions before and after block 3. Do the same few dimensions dominate from block 3 onward?

**Hint.** `hidden_states` from the same forward pass gives you all 13 snapshots; `(h**2).mean(dim=tokens)` then `topk(5)` per layer does the accounting.

**Solution sketch.** ~8 lines: stack hidden states, square, reduce over tokens, `topk` per layer, divide by total. The ledger only commits the norm curve (6.2 up to 740, then 186 after `ln_f`); the dimension-level breakdown is yours to record and compare. If a handful of dimensions carry most of the post-block-3 mass, you have reproduced the outlier-dimension phenomenon on your own machine, and explained why `ln_f` (which the ledger shows collapsing 740 to 186) is load-bearing for the unembedding.
